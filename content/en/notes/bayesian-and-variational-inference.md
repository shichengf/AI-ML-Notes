# Bayesian Inference and Variational Inference

This note answers one question:

> We observe data $x$, but care about the hidden cause $z$ that generated it. How can we infer $z$ and train the generative model?

A VAE contains exactly this problem. Once the problem is clear, priors, posteriors, and variational inference become parts of one story.

## First distinguish $z$, $\theta$, and $\phi$

| Symbol | Meaning in a VAE |
| --- | --- |
| $x$ | Observed data, such as an image of a handwritten digit. |
| $z$ | Latent variables behind one sample, such as stroke width, slant, and other hidden factors. |
| $\theta$ | Parameters of the generative model. In a VAE, these are mainly the decoder network weights. |
| $\phi$ | Parameters of the inference model. In a VAE, these are mainly the encoder network weights. |

$z$ and $\theta$ play very different roles. Each image has its own $z$, while the whole dataset shares one $\theta$. Training changes $\theta$. Given a new image, we want to infer its $z$.

### What does $p_\theta$ mean?

$p_\theta$ is not $p$ multiplied by $\theta$. It denotes a probability distribution controlled by parameters $\theta$.

For example, a decoder may take $z$ and output an image mean $\mu_\theta(z)$. We can define

$$
p_\theta(x\mid z)
=\mathcal{N}\left(x;\mu_\theta(z),\sigma^2 I\right).
$$

This formula says:

> Given $z$, the decoder predicts what the image should look like. The observed image $x$ may contain some noise around that prediction.

Changing the network weights $\theta$ changes $\mu_\theta(z)$ and therefore changes the whole conditional distribution $p_\theta(x\mid z)$.

$p_\theta(z\mid x)$ is the exact posterior implied by the same generative model. It asks the inverse question:

> After seeing image $x$, which values of $z$ were most likely to have generated it?

$p_\theta(z\mid x)$ is not a second neural network that we already possess. It is a distribution determined by the prior $p(z)$ and the decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ through Bayes' rule.

## Generation is easy in the forward direction

A VAE assumes that data are generated by

$$
z\sim p(z),\qquad x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z).
$$

The prior $p(z)$ is usually a standard normal distribution. To generate a new image:

1. Sample $z$ from $p(z)$.
2. Pass $z$ through the decoder.
3. Generate $x$ according to $p_\theta(x\mid z)$.

This direction moves from a hidden cause to an observation, so it is easy to execute.

Training gives us the inverse problem. The dataset contains $x$ but does not reveal the $z$ behind each observation. We need the posterior

$$
p_\theta(z\mid x)
=\frac{p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)}{p_\theta(x)}.
$$

The numerator gives each candidate $z$ a score:

$$
p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z).
$$

$p(z)$ checks whether the candidate was plausible before seeing the image. $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ checks whether the decoder can use it to explain the current $x$. A candidate that scores highly on both should have high posterior probability.

## Why is the posterior difficult?

The difficulty is the denominator

$$
p_\theta(x)
=\int p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz.
$$

It must add the explanations supplied by every possible $z$ before the numerator can be normalized.

If $z$ had only three values with unnormalized scores $2$, $5$, and $3$, the problem would be easy. Divide by their sum $10$ and obtain posterior probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$, and $0.3$.

In a VAE, $z$ is usually a continuous vector with many dimensions. Even a 20 dimensional vector has infinitely many possible values. The decoder is also nonlinear, so this high dimensional integral usually has no formula that we can evaluate directly.

This creates two concrete problems:

1. For a chosen $z$, we can calculate $p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)$. We do not know the total score over all candidates, so we cannot normalize the posterior.
2. Training the decoder requires increasing the data probability $\log p_\theta(x)$. That quantity contains the same difficult integral.

Knowing an unnormalized density is not the same as drawing independent posterior samples directly. MCMC can use an unnormalized density, but it usually requires a separate sampling procedure for each $x$. A VAE is meant to train quickly on many observations, so it needs another approach.

## What variational inference does

Variational inference stops trying to compute $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ directly. It constructs a tractable distribution

$$
q_\phi(z\mid x)
$$

and makes it approximate the exact posterior.

In a VAE, $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ is the distribution defined by the encoder. A common choice is a diagonal Gaussian:

$$
q_\phi(z\mid x)
=\mathcal{N}\left(
z;\mu_\phi(x),
\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))
\right).
$$

The encoder weights are $\phi$. Given an image $x$, it produces $\mu_\phi(x)$ and $\sigma_\phi(x)$ for that image. Each $x$ therefore receives its own Gaussian distribution, while every image shares the same encoder and the same parameters $\phi$.

The ideal objective is

$$
\min_\phi
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(
q_\phi(z\mid x)
\parallel
p_\theta(z\mid x)
\right).
$$

Its intuition is simple:

> Adjust the encoder parameters $\phi$ until the encoder distribution is close to the posterior implied by the generative model.

This is a reverse KL objective. We can sample from $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ and evaluate its density. See [[en/notes/kl-divergence|KL Divergence]] for the distinction between the two KL directions.

## How can we minimize KL without knowing the posterior?

Write Bayes' rule in logarithmic form:

$$
\log p_\theta(z\mid x)
=\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log p_\theta(x).
$$

Substitute it into the KL divergence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\parallel p_\theta(z\mid x)\right)
&=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
\left[
\log q_\phi(z\mid x)-\log p_\theta(x,z)
\right]
+\log p_\theta(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranging gives

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(x)
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(
q_\phi(z\mid x)\parallel p_\theta(z\mid x)
\right),
$$

where

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(x)
=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
\left[
\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)
\right].
$$

These equations state three important facts:

1. $\log p_\theta(x)$ is the data probability that we actually want to increase.
2. The KL divergence is the error between the approximate and exact posterior, and it is nonnegative.
3. The ELBO equals the data probability minus that error, so it is a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x)$.

The exact posterior appears in the identity, but evaluating the ELBO only requires $q_\phi(z\mid x)$, $p(z)$, and $p_\theta(x\mid z)$. We can evaluate or sample from all three. Maximizing the ELBO therefore avoids direct calculation of both $p_\theta(x)$ and $p_\theta(z\mid x)$.

## Why does a VAE need variational inference?

A VAE wants to learn a latent variable generative model

$$
p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z).
$$

If the posterior were tractable, we could infer $z$ for every observation and optimize the data likelihood directly. A neural decoder usually makes both the posterior and the marginal likelihood intractable.

Variational inference addresses both problems:

| What the VAE needs | What variational inference provides |
| --- | --- |
| Infer latent variables $z$ from $x$ | The encoder supplies the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ |
| Train the generative model $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ | The ELBO supplies a tractable training objective |

“Variational” refers to searching for the best distribution within a family of candidate distributions. In a VAE, the encoder and parameters $\phi$ represent that candidate distribution. “Autoencoder” refers to the encoder and decoder network structure.

A VAE is therefore not an ordinary autoencoder with an arbitrary KL term added to it. It begins as a latent variable generative model with an intractable posterior. The encoder and ELBO make inference and training tractable.

### Why does the VAE objective have two terms?

Using

$$
p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z),
$$

the ELBO becomes

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(x)
=
\underbrace{
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
\left[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\right]
}_{\text{make }z\text{ explain }x}
-
\underbrace{
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\parallel p(z)\right)
}_{\text{keep encoded distributions near the prior}}.
$$

The first term is commonly called the reconstruction term. More precisely, it is the expected log likelihood that the decoder assigns to the observation. It becomes a familiar MSE or binary cross entropy only after choosing a particular observation model.

The second term prevents the encoded distributions from scattering arbitrarily through latent space. After training, we want to sample from one shared prior $p(z)$ and generate plausible data. If the encoder places observations in regions that the prior almost never visits, samples from the prior will rarely reach those regions.

### What happens in one training step?

For one image $x$:

1. The encoder computes $\mu_\phi(x)$ and $\sigma_\phi(x)$.
2. Sample $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and set

$$
z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon.
$$

3. The decoder uses $z$ to evaluate $p_\theta(x\mid z)$.
4. The ELBO updates both $\phi$ and $\theta$.

Step two is the reparameterization trick. Randomness is moved into $\epsilon$, so gradients can still pass through $z$ and reach the encoder.

The encoder does not memorize a fixed $z$ for one image. It learns a shared mapping from any $x$ to an approximate posterior distribution. This is called amortized inference. After training, a new observation needs only one encoder pass instead of a new iterative inference procedure.

## Connect the roles one last time

| Object | Role |
| --- | --- |
| $p(z)$ | Specifies where latent variables are sampled during generation. |
| $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ | The decoder. It turns a latent variable into a distribution over data. |
| $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ | The exact posterior implied by the generative model. We want it, but usually cannot calculate it. |
| $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ | The encoder. It approximates the exact posterior with a tractable distribution. |
| ELBO | A tractable objective that trains both encoder and decoder without calculating the exact posterior. |

Variational inference is more than replacing one distribution with a Gaussian. It turns an intractable posterior inference problem into an optimization problem that can be trained with gradients. A VAE uses an encoder to turn that inference procedure into one neural network forward pass.
